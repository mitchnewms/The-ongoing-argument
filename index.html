Relationship Adviser

When you write for a sitcom you have twenty two minutes.

Twenty two minutes to set up a conflict, build it, and resolve it. Every week. Tell a story. Resolve the conflict. Done.

Then I became a relationship coach.

And I discovered that the same conflicts I was resolving on screen in twenty two minutes were playing out in real relationships for years. Sometimes decades. The same fight. The same lines. No resolution. No end in sight.

Just to be continued.

So I married what I knew as a screenwriter with what I learned as a coach. And created a process that helps couples do what TV writers do every week.

Find the solution.

This is a couples process. That’s what it’s built for, and that’s where it has the power to change things long term.

But if right now, your partner isn’t in a place to do this with you, that doesn’t stop you.

It just means you’re starting with you.

There’s real value in that. A chance to understand yourself better, and your partner better, even on your own. And often, what shifts in you has a way of shifting what’s possible between you — sometimes more than either of you would expect.

It is no coincidence that you found each other. We often seek the one person who will ultimately challenge us to reconsider the parts of ourselves we fear the most by activating those parts. And in kind and in reaction we then know exactly what to say to share that gift back for them. And I do mean gift. Change. Healing. Both tend to sting a bit before we get to the good parts.

Here is what is important to know. Most couples want the same things. What differs is how they were each taught to achieve them. The fight is not evidence that something is broken. It is evidence that something needs to be broken open to reveal one's greatest capacity for loving. Underneath every ongoing argument is an unmet need that has never been named and clearly not met.

Name it. Meet it. And watch what magic becomes possible.

Before
LOCATION: Kitchen, Sunday morning. ALEX (loading dishwasher, back turned) You never put things where they go. JORDAN I just woke up. ALEX That's not what I'm talking about. JORDAN Then say what you're talking about. ALEX (turns around) Every single time. I ask for one thing. JORDAN You didn't ask. You announced. ALEX Oh so now I'm bad at communicating too? JORDAN I'm going back to bed. ALEX Of course you are. That's what you do. JORDAN (stops) What does that mean? ALEX It means you disappear when things get hard. JORDAN I disappear? You've been cold to me for three days. ALEX Because you don't listen. JORDAN I can't listen to what you never say. ALEX I shouldn't have to spell it out every time. JORDAN Then I don't know how to help you. ALEX (quietly) That's exactly the problem.
Better
LOCATION: Kitchen, Sunday morning. ALEX (loading dishwasher, pauses) Can I say something without it becoming a thing? JORDAN I can try. What's up? ALEX When things aren't where I expect them I get anxious. I know that's mine. But it's been happening a lot and I haven't known how to bring it up. JORDAN Okay. I didn't know it was landing that way. ALEX I think I've been needing to feel like we're on the same team about the space. Not rules. Just — shared. JORDAN That makes sense. I think I sometimes zone out and don't notice. Not because I don't care. I just move differently. ALEX I know you do. I don't need you to be me about it. I just need to feel like it matters to you that it matters to me. JORDAN It does. I think I need reminders sometimes. Gentle ones. ALEX (small smile) I can do gentle. JORDAN Thank you for saying this instead of going quiet. ALEX Thank you for not getting defensive. JORDAN I almost did. ALEX Me too.

Step 11 — The Bridge
The Forgetting and Remembering
From time to time, everyone forgets.

They drift from their center. From their knowing. From the wholeness that was never actually lost. And then something calls to them — and in the return to the present moment, they remember.

The ongoing argument you just worked through is one example of the forgetting. An old program ran. Old roles appeared. Old needs went unmet again. The work you just did together is the remembering.

But you will forget again. Not because the work failed. Because you are human.

The Tools Apply Everywhere
These are not relationship tools. These are human tools.

The tools practiced here were prompted by one relationship. But look at what those tools actually are. Listening beneath the surface. Identifying what is truly needed. Owning your own experience. Making requests instead of demands. Getting out of story and into solution.

Every relationship in your life asks for the same thing. Colleagues. Parents. Children. The self. The programming that showed up here shows up there too. Different names. Different stages. The same disconnect. The same unmet need underneath it. The same invitation to something better.

      These aren’t just things to remember for this one exercise. You’re going to have communication breakdowns again, with this person, and with others.       To Do
      • Notice your energy shifting before you speak. That’s the moment to pause, not push through.
      • Know the difference between a reaction and a response. We want response, not reaction.
      • If you need a minute, take it. Ask for it out loud. “I need five minutes to clear my head, then I want to come back to this, is that okay?”
      • Reflect and ask a clarifying question. “I want to make sure I heard you right, are you saying…” This isn’t about defending, it’s about understanding their experience, even if it’s not your own.
      • Remember, this is a conversation, not a confrontation.
      • Don’t take the bait.
      If you’re the one bringing something up:
      • Come to your partner, don’t come at them.
      • Use I-language. You’re having a feeling, an experience. Avoid you-language that can land as judgment or blame.
      • Try starting with “the story I tell myself is…” Starting there keeps you in your own experience instead of making a claim about your partner, so they can hear it as information, not an attack.
      • Keep it simple. “I’d like to talk to you about something that’s come up for me. Is this a good time?”
      • Be okay if the answer is no. If you genuinely want to be heard, you want your partner to have ears on their heart, truly present enough to receive it, not just physically there.

      • Committed to not taking the bait
